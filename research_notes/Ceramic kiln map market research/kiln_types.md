# Taxonomy of Ceramic Kiln and Firing Types, and How Common Each Is

Research date: 2026-10-04. Focus: US studio potters, with notes on Japan and Australia. Purpose: help decide which categories a kiln-location map should include, and which are rare enough that potters go looking for access.

## What are the main kiln/firing categories, and how do they differ (fuel, cone, duration, crew, cost, location)?

### Takeaway
Kilns sort along three axes: **fuel/heat source** (electric, gas/propane, wood, oil/waste oil, combustibles in a pit or barrel), **atmosphere** (oxidation, reduction, or "atmospheric", meaning vapor or ash from salt, soda or wood lands on the pots), and **draft/structure** (updraft, downdraft, cross-draft; for wood, tube/anagama, chambered/noborigama, train, groundhog, bourry box, catenary arch, fast-fire). Electric oxidation (usually cone 6) is the everyday baseline. Gas reduction (cone 10) is the standard for university and community studios. Wood, salt and soda are specialist "atmospheric" firings that take dedicated kilns, long firings and, for wood, crews.

### Cited Findings

**Overall taxonomy / prevalence baseline**
- Ceramic Arts Network's kiln guide lists electric, gas, oil, wood, raku, salt/soda, pit, test and front-loading kilns, and says "Potters use electric kilns more than any other type of kiln." It also calls electric kilns easy to operate, with many commercial options. — [Ceramic Arts Network, A Guide to Ceramic Kilns](https://ceramicartsnetwork.org/freebies/guide/a-guide-to-ceramic-kilns)
- Gas kilns are described as the most popular kind of *fuel-burning* kiln today. — [Ceramic Arts Network, gas kiln firing category](https://ceramicartsnetwork.org/category/firing-techniques/gas-kiln-firing/) (as summarized in search results)
- Draft classes: **updraft** (heat enters at the base and exits through a flue at the top; easier to build but often heats unevenly), **downdraft** (a flue at the bottom pulls heat down, giving more even temperatures) and **cross-draft** (heat moves horizontally across the chamber). — [LibreTexts / University of the Pacific Ceramics 9.05](https://human.libretexts.org/Courses/University_of_the_Pacific/Ceramics/09%3A_The_Firing_Process/9.05%3A_Types_of_Kilns_and_Locations)

**Electric (oxidation)**
- Cone 6 is the common electric target. Practitioners advise buying a kiln rated to cone 10 even when firing to cone 6, because elements last longer when they reach temperature faster. Elements last about 100 firings at cone 6 and about 50 at cone 10. — [The Pottery Wheel, kiln element life](https://thepotterywheel.com/how-long-do-kiln-elements-last/); [Skutt, element tips](https://skutt.com/tag/elements/)
- Electric kilns cannot do reduction. Most cone 10 work is fired in reduction, and "pretty much anything you can do in cone 10 oxidation you can do at cone 6." — [Ceramic Arts Daily forum, cone 9/10 electric](https://community.ceramicartsdaily.org/topic/20596-cone-9-10-electric/)

**Gas reduction (natural gas / propane; updraft or downdraft)**
- A typical cone 10 reduction schedule is an 8–10 hour overnight preheat, then 4–5+ hours to cone 06 with body reduction around cone 012–09, then 1–2+ hours to cone 10. Another reported kiln takes 17 hours in total. — [Ceramic Arts Daily forum, gas kiln threads](https://community.ceramicartsdaily.org/topic/18959-gas-kiln-draft-question/) (as summarized in search results)
- Rental price example: a 32 cu ft Bailey downdraft gas kiln rents for $255 per cone 10 firing. Owner-reported propane costs per firing range from about $7–8 to $20–25 (older forum data, small kilns). — search-result summary of [Ceramic Arts Daily forum](https://community.ceramicartsdaily.org/topic/337-homebuilt-gas-kiln-need-advice/page/2/) and a studio rate sheet. Treat these figures as anecdotal.
- University example (Univ. of Kentucky): four Skutt electric kilns, one Cone Art electric, a 36 ft³ Geil electric car kiln, a 35 ft³ Geil gas kiln and a 60 ft³ soda kiln. This is a typical university mix: many electric, one or two gas, one atmospheric. — [UK Fine Arts Ceramics Studios](https://finearts.uky.edu/taxonomy/term/196)

**Wood firing (general)**
- An anagama is a single firing chamber with a firebox at one end and a flue at the other. Brought to Japan by Korean potters around the 5th century. Firings run anywhere from 48 hours to 12 or more days, depending on volume. — [Wikipedia, Anagama kiln](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anagama_kiln)
- A noborigama is a chambered climbing kiln built on a slope, used in Japan since the 17th century. Each chamber is stoked independently, which gives different atmospheres within one firing. — [Wikipedia, Anagama kiln (noborigama section)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anagama_kiln); [LibreTexts 9.05](https://human.libretexts.org/Courses/University_of_the_Pacific/Ceramics/09%3A_The_Firing_Process/9.05%3A_Types_of_Kilns_and_Locations)
- Bizen (one of Japan's "Six Old Kilns": Shigaraki, Bizen, Tanba, Echizen, Seto, Tokoname) still fires in noborigama and anagama with red pine. Firings last 10–14 days at 1200–1300 °C. — [Wikipedia, Bizen ware](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bizen_ware); [Japanese Embassy UK PDF](https://www.uk.emb-japan.go.jp/files/100342768.pdf)
- **Train kiln**: designed by John Neely at Utah State University, based on Japanese kilns. It has an elongated horizontal ware chamber, an elevated firebox and a stepped grate. Air enters on top of the wood and is drawn down through it, which gives heavy ash surfaces "using less wood and man power than a tube kiln." The design is public domain and has spread to "so many countries Neely's lost track." — [Utah State Magazine, The Train Kiln](https://utahstatemagazine.usu.edu/culture/campus-scene-the-train-kiln/); [Tales of a Red Clay Rambler ep. 260](https://talesofaredclayrambler.com/episodes/260-john-neely-on-the-development-of-the-train-kiln)
- **Groundhog kiln**: a 19th-century Southern US variant of the climbing/tunnel kiln. It is semi-subterranean, built into a low hillside with earth-banked walls, a sunken firebox inside the door, a long low ware bed and a chimney uphill. It was used for alkaline-glazed and salt-glazed stoneware, and is related in form to Chinese "dragon kilns." — [Wikipedia, Catawba Valley Pottery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catawba_Valley_Pottery); [Folkstreams, The Kiln and Its Firing](https://folkstreams.net/contexts/the-kiln-and-its-firing); [Smithsonian Folklife, Burlon Craig](https://folklife.si.edu/magazine/burlon-craig-catawba-valley-pottery)
- **Bourry box**: a firebox type in which wood is stoked onto hobs, so it must be cut to precise lengths. It can make wood firing "feasible" for potters who found it too unpredictable or labor-intensive, and it gives glazed surfaces "only slightly affected by kiln ash." It is often combined with soda (e.g., "double bourry box wood/soda kiln"). — [Pine Mills (Gary Hatcher), Chariot of Fire](https://pinemills.com/Articles/chariotoffire.html) (as summarized in search results); [woodkiln Google Group, Bourry firebox throat](https://groups.google.com/g/woodkiln/c/4anekeAwKPI)
- **Catenary arch**: a self-supporting arch that holds its shape over repeated heating cycles. It is described as "smaller and more efficient than anagama or noborigama designs." — [LibreTexts 9.05](https://human.libretexts.org/Courses/University_of_the_Pacific/Ceramics/09%3A_The_Firing_Process/9.05%3A_Types_of_Kilns_and_Locations)
- **Fast-fire (Fred Olsen)**: a downdraft design that reaches cone 10 in about 8–12 hours with little wood. Its small footprint suits a backyard or pasture. **Manabigama (John Thies)**: fires to cone 10 in about 8 hours. Olsen's *The Kiln Book* (4th ed.) is the standard reference on kiln design. — [Ceramic Arts Daily forum, first wood kiln build](https://community.ceramicartsdaily.org/topic/40861-first-wood-fired-kiln-build-problem-stalling/); [The Kiln Book listing](https://nmclay.com/812221869-the-kiln-book-4th-edition)
- Simon Levin's World Wood Kiln Map color-codes six wood-kiln classes: anagama, fast fire, train, cross-draft, groundhog and noborigama. This is a ready-made category scheme already used by practitioners. — [simonlevin.com/map](https://simonlevin.com/map)

**Salt and soda (vapor-glazed / atmospheric, usually gas- or wood-fueled)**
- Both fire unglazed or partly glazed ware to high temperature, typically cone 10, and introduce a sodium source through burner ports. The vapor forms a sodium-alumino-silicate glaze on the clay. — [Digitalfire, Salt and Soda Firing](https://digitalfire.com/glossary/salt+and+soda+firing)
- Salt (NaCl) releases HCl and chlorine. Soda (sodium carbonate/bicarbonate) releases CO2 and water vapor. Salt firing dates to 12th-century Rhineland. Modern soda firing grew out of Alfred University research in 1971–74. "Many salt kilns have been shut down in urban settings or areas that have stringent pollution laws." — [Ceramics Monthly, Techno File: Salt vs. Soda](https://ceramicartsnetwork.org/ceramics-monthly/ceramics-monthly-article/techno-file--salt-vs.-soda-firing)
- Both need a **dedicated kiln**. Walls, burners and refractories deteriorate faster, especially with salt. Digitalfire notes potters "pay considerable amounts" for workshops to learn these methods. — [Digitalfire](https://digitalfire.com/glossary/salt+and+soda+firing)
- Don Reitz brought salt glazing into Alfred's curriculum in 1959, and it spread to other US university programs. University safety guidance recommends soda instead, and allows salt only outdoors with a canopy hood and tall chimney. — [Wikipedia, Salt glaze pottery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salt_glaze_pottery); [CSB/SJU EHS ceramics](https://csbsju.edu/environmental-health-safety/programs/studio-and-shop-safety/arts-theater/ceramics)
- Evidence conflicts on salt's emissions. Some measurements reportedly show salt firing is "as clean or cleaner than fuel reduction firing." — [Digitalfire](https://digitalfire.com/glossary/salt+and+soda+firing)

**Raku**
- Work goes into the kiln and is brought to about 1800–1900 °F. Propane raku kilns reach about 1850 °F in 30–40 minutes. A full firing takes about 45–90 minutes. — [Ceramic Arts Network, Tips on Purchasing a Raku Kiln](https://ceramicartsnetwork.org/daily/article/Helpful-Tips-on-Purchasing-a-Raku-Kiln); [Raku firing calculator](https://usecalcpro.com/crafts/raku-firing-calculator) (as summarized in search results)

**Pit / barrel / saggar**
- Pit firing: a lazy flame heats for a couple of hours, then the pit or barrel is covered so the ware soaks in carbon. About 4–5 hours in total. — [PCC Galleries, process](https://www.pcc.edu/galleries/2010/08/01/multiple-artists-our-work-the-process/)
- Barrel firing was developed as an alternative to long pit firings. It uses a steel barrel of sawdust, straw, wood, metallic colorants and pots, fired over several hours. — [CraftCourses, saggar/barrel/raku workshops](https://marketplace.craftcourses.com/courses/saggar-barrel-and-raku-firing-weekend-workshops-in-beautiful-devon)
- Saggar firing in a raku kiln holds 1600–1700 °F for about 20 minutes. Saggars can also go into gas or electric kilns, so this is a technique more than a kiln type. — [Ceramic Arts Network, Saggar firing in a raku kiln](https://ceramicartsnetwork.org/daily/article/Tips-for-Making-Firing-and-Finishing-Saggars-and-Saggar-Fired-Pottery-in-a-Raku-Kiln)

**Oil / waste vegetable oil (WVO)**
- WVO drip burners are stepped I-beam plates. Oil feeds by gravity from an elevated 50-gallon drum, vaporizes and ignites. The kiln is started on wood to about cone 1, then fired on oil to cone 10. WVO is pitched as a renewable waste fuel. — [Ceramic Arts Network, Firing with Vegetable Oil](https://ceramicartsnetwork.org/ceramic-recipes/recipe/Firing-with-Vegetable-Oil); [Ceramics Monthly, Clay Culture: WVO Firings](https://ceramicartsnetwork.org/ceramics-monthly/ceramics-monthly-article/Clay-Culture-WVO-Firings-155196)

**Hybrids**
- Wood/soda hybrids are common (e.g., a double bourry box wood/soda kiln). Wood/oil hybrids exist (WVO kilns started on wood). Studio Potter mentions an "anagama hybrid." — [Studio Potter, So You Want Wood Fire](https://studiopotter.org/so-you-want-wood-fire); [Ceramic Arts Network WVO](https://ceramicartsnetwork.org/ceramic-recipes/recipe/Firing-with-Vegetable-Oil)

### Inferences
- **Summary matrix** (synthesized from the findings above; cone and duration figures are typical, not universal):

| Category | Fuel | Typical cone / temp | Firing time | Crew | Typical location | Access difficulty |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Electric oxidation | Electricity | cone 06–6 (to 10 possible) | ~8–12 h plus cooling | 1, unattended | Home, community, school | Ubiquitous |
| Gas reduction | Natural gas / propane | cone 10 (also cone 6) | ~12–17 h | 1–2 | Community studios, universities, some private | Common in studios, uncommon at home |
| Soda | Gas or wood + soda ash | cone 10 | ~12–24 h | 2–4 | Universities, community studios, private | Moderately rare |
| Salt | Gas or wood + NaCl | cone 6–10 | ~12–24 h | 2–4 | Rural private, some schools | Rare (regulatory pressure) |
| Wood: anagama / noborigama / groundhog | Wood | cone 10–12, 1200–1300 °C | 2–14 days | 10–25 in shifts | Rural private, universities, art centers | Rare, seek-out |
| Wood: train / cross-draft / catenary / bourry box | Wood (± soda) | cone 10 | ~1–3 days | 4–15 | Universities, art centers, private | Rare, seek-out |
| Wood: Fast-fire / Manabigama | Wood | cone 10 | ~8–12 h | 1–3 | Backyard / private | Rare, but personal |
| Raku | Propane (sometimes wood/electric) | ~1800–1900 °F (~cone 06) | 45–90 min per load | 2+ | Community studios, workshops, outdoors | Moderate; widely offered as events |
| Pit / barrel / saggar | Combustibles (sawdust, wood, etc.) | low fire, ~1200–1700 °F | 4 h–overnight | 1–several | Backyard, workshops, festivals | Easy (DIY), event-based |
| Oil / WVO | Waste oil (often wood start) | to cone 10 | ~1–2 days | several | Private rural, experimental | Very rare |

- Cone figures for electric (06–6) and the soda/salt, train and raku durations come from common practice, not from one cited source. Spot-check them before publishing.
- For a map, Simon Levin's six wood classes (anagama, fast-fire, train, cross-draft, groundhog, noborigama), plus bourry box and catenary, are a good wood sub-taxonomy. Top-level categories could be: Electric, Gas reduction, Soda, Salt, Wood (with subtypes), Raku, Pit/Barrel/Saggar, Oil/Other.

### Gaps
- No single authoritative source gives cone, duration, crew and cost for every type side by side. The matrix is a synthesis.
- I found no published per-firing cost data (wood cords, propane gallons) for wood, soda or salt kilns. The Watershed 2026 kiln-cost PDF returned 404.
- Detailed bourry box history (Emile Bourry, popularized by Daniel Rhodes's *Kilns*) is not confirmed by the sources fetched.
- Catenary-arch kilns are a structural form fired with gas, wood or soda, not a fuel category. The sources do not clearly separate the two.

## Which types are rare or hard to access, and which are ubiquitous?

### Takeaway
Electric kilns are ubiquitous: most common overall, in home studios, and in nearly every community and school studio. Gas reduction is standard in institutions but uncommon at home. Wood, salt and (less so) soda are scarce. They live in universities, art centers and rural private sites, so potters actively seek out firings by joining crews, entering lotteries or renting space. These atmospheric types are the strongest "search for access" categories for a map.

### Cited Findings
- Electric is the most-used kiln type. — [Ceramic Arts Network guide](https://ceramicartsnetwork.org/freebies/guide/a-guide-to-ceramic-kilns)
- Electric kilns are very popular with hobby and small-scale potters, and gas kilns "are not as common today as electric ones." — [The Pottery Wheel, 11 Types of Kiln](https://thepotterywheel.com/types-of-kiln/); [KilnControl, Types of Kilns](https://www.kilncontrol.com/blog/types-of-kilns/) (as summarized in search results)
- Community studios that offer atmospheric kilns advertise them as differentiators. Clay Pittsburgh has raku, soda, electric and gas reduction. Wesleyan Potters has 2 gas, 7 electric, 1 raku and 1 soda. Alison Palmer Studio has a propane soda kiln and a wood kiln. — [Clay Pittsburgh, Kilns and Firing](https://claypittsburgh.org/kilns-and-firing/); [CT Clay Guild studio directory](https://www.ctclayguild.com/ct-studios)
- US wood kilns: "there are hundreds of kilns in the United States," including teaching kilns at craft centers, universities, community colleges and collectives. "Twenty years ago, there weren't as many wood-fired kilns as there are today." — [Studio Potter, So You Want Wood Fire](https://studiopotter.org/so-you-want-wood-fire)
- Wood kilns break down by ownership into school-owned (mostly for students), community-based with public workshops (e.g., East Creek Anagama, Oregon) and privately owned (built to fire one artist's work). — [American Craft Council, Kilns That Build Community](https://craftcouncil.org/articles/kilns-that-build-community/) (via search summary; direct fetch returned 403)
- Demand exceeds supply at public wood kilns. Hambidge Center (GA) fills 15 public spots by lottery about 3 months ahead, with 10 more for invited experienced wood-firers. Art Center of the Bluegrass (KY) says "interest in the anagama kiln is high" and that "all work may not get into the firing." — [Hambidge, Anagama Firings](https://www.hambidge.org/anagama-kiln-firings); [Art Center of the Bluegrass, Dragon Fire Anagama](https://artcenterky.org/registration/anagama/)
- Salt kilns are shrinking in urban areas because of pollution rules. — [Ceramics Monthly, Techno File](https://ceramicartsnetwork.org/ceramics-monthly/ceramics-monthly-article/techno-file--salt-vs.-soda-firing)
- Salt and soda are niche. Potters pay considerable amounts for workshops. — [Digitalfire](https://digitalfire.com/glossary/salt+and+soda+firing)

### Inferences
- Rough rarity ladder, most to least accessible: electric > gas reduction > raku (event-based, widely offered) > pit/barrel (DIY) > soda > wood (small/fast-fire, train) > salt > large anagama/noborigama/groundhog > oil/WVO.
- "Seek-out" categories that justify a map: wood (all subtypes), soda, salt, and to a lesser degree gas reduction (for potters with only electric at home) and raku (event discovery). Electric is so common that mapping it adds little, except for kiln-rental or firing-service listings.

### Gaps
- No quantitative US data on the share of home, community or university studios that own each kiln type.

## Is there data on how many wood, soda or salt kilns exist, and what share of potters use each type?

### Takeaway
There are **no official counts**. The best US figure is qualitative ("hundreds" of wood kilns, per Studio Potter). The main registry is Simon Levin's crowdsourced World Wood Kiln Map, which covers wood only and has no published count. I found no survey (Ceramic Arts Network, NCECA, Studio Potter) giving the share of potters by kiln type, and no count of soda or salt kilns. This gap is itself a market signal.

### Cited Findings
- "There are no official statistics on the number of wood kilns in the United States." Simon Levin crowdsources a searchable map at simonlevin.com/map that shows their wide geographic spread. — [American Craft Council, Kilns That Build Community](https://craftcouncil.org/articles/kilns-that-build-community/) (via search summary)
- Levin's map is described as a "public resource of wood fired kilns around the world." Kiln owners submit by email: name, type, size in cubic feet, construction date, contact and website. Six color-coded types. The page shows no total count. — [simonlevin.com/map](https://simonlevin.com/map)
- Levin has built an anagama, a cross-draft and a train kiln, and has built kilns for many colleges in the US and abroad. — [Illinois Times](https://www.illinoistimes.com/special-issues/this-artist-harnesses-earth-wind-fire-and-water-17810456/); [NPR Illinois, 2026-04-23](https://www.nprillinois.org/arts-life/2026-04-23/simon-levin-pottery-sale-coming-up-at-his-wood-fire-kiln-studio-in-pawnee-illinois)
- "Hundreds" of US wood kilns, and growing over the past 20 years. — [Studio Potter](https://studiopotter.org/so-you-want-wood-fire)
- Australia: the 1996 Australian Woodfire Survey (Strathnairn Ceramics Association, 8th National Ceramics Conference, Canberra) gives no total count. It notes anagama/tube kilns had become "more common in the past ten years." The first Australian anagama was built by Milton Moon in the 1960s. — [Owen Rye, Australian Woodfire Survey 1996](https://www.owenrye.com/written-by-owen-rye/australian-woodfire-survey-1996)
- Australia today: Montsalvat's Festival of Fire & Clay (June 15–21, 2026) featured anagama, Roman kiln, gas reduction, hikidashi raku, pit and salt firings. — [Montsalvat / WeTeachMe listing](https://weteachme.com/classes/1046965-montsalvat-festival-of-fire-and-clay-15-21-june)
- Japan: Bizen is "the last area producing this style of pottery in any quantity" and still uses anagama/noborigama. I found no kiln count. — [Wikipedia, Bizen ware](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bizen_ware)
- Ceramic Arts Network gives only the qualitative "electric more than any other type" and no percentages. — [Ceramic Arts Network guide](https://ceramicartsnetwork.org/freebies/guide/a-guide-to-ceramic-kilns)

### Inferences
- "Hundreds" of US wood kilns is plausibly in the low-to-mid hundreds for public or semi-public ones, but this is unverified.
- The lack of any registry for soda and salt kilns, and the reliance on one person's emailed submissions for wood, suggests the map would be filling a real data gap.

### Gaps
- No counts of US or global soda or salt kilns. No NCECA, Ceramic Arts Network or Studio Potter survey on kiln-type share was found. The Ceramic Arts Network reader surveys, if they exist, are not public.
- I could not get the number of entries on Levin's map. The map is embedded and the page text gives no count. One option is to contact Levin, or scrape the embedded map data.
- UK figures were not found. The Anagama Wood-Fire Society, the UK's Craft Potters Association and NCECA wood-fire sessions are leads.
- Japanese kiln counts (e.g., the number of Bizen anagama/noborigama) were not found in English sources.

## How do potters participate in atmospheric firings (crew slots, paying per shelf or cubic foot, workshops)?

### Takeaway
There are four main models: (1) **pay a share fee** (flat fee per share or per volume, e.g., $125 for 2,000 in³, or $325 per share); (2) **sweat equity**, where crew hours earn points that set loading priority; (3) **lottery or invitation** for oversubscribed public kilns; and (4) **workshops, residencies and apprenticeships**, from weekend workshops to one- or two-year live-work apprenticeships. Nearly all models require stoking shifts (often 8 hours each, several per person) plus help with wood prep, loading, unloading and cleanup.

### Cited Findings
- **Hambidge Center (GA) anagama**: $125 participation fee covering up to 2,000 cubic inches, plus $0.10 per additional cubic inch. 25 participants (15 by lottery about 3 months ahead, 10 invited). Each must stoke at least one shift. Fires Friday to Sunday to cone 10, unloads the next weekend. Work must be cone 10 clay and bisqued. — [Hambidge, Anagama Firing Schedule + Registration](https://www.hambidge.org/anagama-kiln-firings)
- **Art Center of the Bluegrass (Danville, KY) Dragon Fire anagama**: a 6-day firing. 1 hour of work earns 1 point, and points set loading order (30+ = first group, 20–29 second, 10–19 third, 0–9 remaining space). Points can be bought for $25 each. Shifts are staffed by 1 person (candling), then 3 per shift (days 2–3), then 4 per shift (days 4–6). — [Art Center of the Bluegrass](https://artcenterky.org/registration/anagama/)
- Other examples: a $325-per-share firing fee; up to 10 cubic feet of work per participant; usually four 8-hour firing shifts per person; all participants help with prep, loading, firing and cleanup. — [Gustin Ceramics](https://gustinceramics.com/?p=377); [Tyler Park Arts, Wood Kiln Firing](https://tylerparkarts.org/woodkiln-firing) (as summarized in search results; which fee belongs to which site was not separately verified)
- Access ranges from "weekend workshops and private firings to multiyear apprenticeships," including residencies, assistantships and work-study at craft centers. "Many wood-fire ceramists own kilns and invite others to participate—for a fee, for work exchange, or a combination of both." — [Studio Potter, So You Want Wood Fire](https://studiopotter.org/so-you-want-wood-fire)
- Festivals and intensives are another entry point (e.g., Montsalvat's 7-day Fire & Clay festival in Australia with anagama and salt firings). — [Montsalvat](https://weteachme.com/classes/1046965-montsalvat-festival-of-fire-and-clay-15-21-june)
- Gas kiln rental example: $255 per cone 10 firing for a 32 cu ft Bailey downdraft. — search-result summary (studio rate sheet, source not verified)

### Inferences
- A map listing should capture the participation model (fee per share or volume, work-for-space, lottery, invitation-only, workshop/residency), cone, firing length, required shifts, typical schedule (often spring and fall), and whether outsiders are accepted. These are the fields potters need to judge access.
- Fees are low ($100–$350 per share) relative to the labor asked (4+ eight-hour shifts). The binding constraint is slots and timing, not money, so firing calendars and sign-up dates may matter more than prices.

### Gaps
- No aggregated data on typical share prices or crew sizes across many kilns. The examples are anecdotal (GA, KY and a few others).
- Per-shelf pricing for soda or salt firings at community studios was not found in this pass.
